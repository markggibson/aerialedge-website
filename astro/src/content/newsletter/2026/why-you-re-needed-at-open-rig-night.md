## WELCOME TO THE PARTY

## A WEEK TONIGHT, A TICKET GETS YOU IN

I stumbled on an iconic [Audience with Billy Connolly](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/944d58ba5c6?contactId={{contact.id}}&v=Npq9XxFGmeQ&t=1189s&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing) video on an odyssey through YouTube this week...the Glasgow entertainment world of today is a whole lot different to the one he hilariously describes.

The young Big Yin's formative social experiences weren't as far-fetched as they sound now – wandering the streets listening for a party to crash, where you'd then be required to "do your turn". And if someone sang your song or performed your party trick – your "turn", the one thing you believed you could do reasonably well – you were stuffed. Either risk something you couldn't do, or get out before it really was your turn.

Open Rig Night actually has some similarities. People who come here for a good time are invited to do their turn on the stage on what could reasonably be called a party, it's such a good atmosphere.

But there are infinite possibilities with circus arts. There's a good chance that someone else will use the same apparatus as you, and that's just fine. Everyone does what they're good at with that apparatus, and their act is therefore unique and special.

A week tonight, it's a party at Aerial Edge, and you're invited to come see the show. No need to come up with a story about some guy called Jimmy who said you could come, [a ticket](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/3b057aadc69?contactId={{contact.id}}&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing) will get you through the door. And while you won't be asked to do your turn, you will be playing a crucial role (see the article below for the proof).

There will be plenty of people upside down, but not behind the telly in a stupor, like Jimmy in Billy's story.

## NO FAKING IT, YOU MAKE IT

## WHY OPEN RIG NIGHT'S PERFORMERS NEED AN AUDIENCE

The Royal College of Music in London once built itself a fake audience, which isn't as mad as it might at first sound. It was 24 people on film, and they could fidget, cough, applaud, rise for a standing ovation or, if the occasion called for it, boo. A building full of musicians went to all that trouble because practising in an empty room can't tell you how it feels when you perform in a room that is full.

That feeling is a powerful reason for anyone to put their name down for Open Rig Night. The performers already know their sequence, having run it in class until the crash mats could probably brace themselves at the appropriate moments.

They want to perform at Open Rig Night to find out how it feels when a room full of people does a collective sharp intake of breath at the drop and starts clapping before the final pose has quite finished. You can't fake that reaction, because circus performance isn't always perfect, even if it looks perfect to the audience. Things don't always go exactly to the timing, moves might be adapted.

Outbursts of spontaneous joy, relief, appreciation, surprise are what makes it special. None of that can happen without bums on seats, and next Saturday one of those bums could be yours.

Football has some evidence that being in the audience can positively affect the performance too.

In a normal season, nobody in the stands leaps out of their seat, runs on to the pitch, dodges past the defence and hits the back of the net. Yet after crowds were locked out in 2020, researchers at the [German Sport University Cologne](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/8ec7e7d1a32?contactId={{contact.id}}&id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0248590&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing) looked at more than 1,000 top-flight matches played to empty stands and found the home side's advantage in shots had roughly halved. That showed that when people were in the seats, it could change how the players played, and how the matches ended.

Just think. The person on the rig could do a little better by having you there.

When they look out from the rig, what they see and hear could have a bit of you cheering in it, and the feeling they enjoy will stay in their heart for a very long time.

You don't have to be a performer to make your mark on Open Rig Night, but you do have to be there.

## ALL CHANGE AT THE EDGE

## BUY YOUR PASS NOW, THEN PLAN YOUR DAY OF MIX-UPS

There's a version of October Edge where you do flying trapeze all day, or a variety of aerial activities in every time slot, and some people are bound to look at that and think: Yaaaassss! Then, by the end of the first afternoon, their shoulders will be filing formal complaints and they'll be asking to switch to trampoline in the last workshop of the day.

Variety is not only the spice of life, it's the antidote to muscle fatigue at the October Edge.

Before booking opens for the arts in the time slots next Saturday at 1pm, you want to do two things: (1) buy your pass, and (2) relish the task of planning your best route through the schedule.

At 1pm you need to be clicking 'book now' on your chosen workshops, not speed-typing in your card details while someone else snags the last place on aerial hammock.

When you plan your workshops, mix it up. Tiredness mostly stays where you put it. Pulling together [52 studies](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/8a189adf35a?contactId={{contact.id}}&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing), a team led by David Behm at Memorial University of Newfoundland found that wearing out one set of muscles made almost no difference to the strength and power of the others.

Think about that. A morning on rope and flying trapeze leaves your legs ready to bounce on the trampoline at 3.45pm while your arms have a tea break.

So rather than fly, fly, fly and fly, one route might run acrobatics at 10am, flying trapeze before lunch, silks after it and partner acro to finish. Another might go rope, Chinese pole, handstands and trampoline.

Whichever four you pick are yours for all three days, so it's worth handing your body and your brain a few different problems to solve.

In a classic 1979 [experiment](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/57ea6053c7d?contactId={{contact.id}}&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing), people who practised three movements in a jumbled order looked worse at the time yet remembered them better 10 days later. There's a cheering thought when one of your four arts is the one you feel like you're worst at.

It's worth giving every slot a plan B, because some workshops could fill before you get to them, and one full slot can shuffle the rest of your day.

But don't be dismayed. In February 2014 a strike shut 171 of London's 270 Tube stations for two days, and thousands of commuters had to find another way to work. Economists from Cambridge and Oxford followed their Oyster cards afterwards and found that about [one in 20 kept the new route](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/4f2e73c907f?contactId={{contact.id}}&redirectedFrom=fulltext&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing), because it turned out to be better than the one they'd been taking all along.

So if straps and loops is full by the time you get there, you're on the strike route. By Sunday you might be the one telling anyone who'll stand still that Chinese pole was the best thing that happened all weekend.

## WHERE DOES YOUR FOCUS GO?

## DAYTIME CLASSES ARE BACK, ON DIFFERENT DAYS EACH WEEK

Next Wednesday it will be 10.55am, the washing machine will have given up beeping about something it finished an hour earlier, and the laptop on the kitchen table will have at least three tabs open that are not relevant to your official to-do list. That's roughly the moment to shut the lid, because by 11.30am you could be upside down in a set of silks.

Researchers at the University of Bristol would recognise the kitchen. In their [study](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/f62f649d8e8?contactId={{contact.id}}&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing) of about 200 office workers, people rated their mood and their work higher on days they'd exercised during the day than on days they hadn't, including managing their workload and getting on with colleagues. On the days without exercise, their sense of calm got steadily worse as the day went on.

We can help you perform better at work with a smorgasbord of daytime classes if your days can bend a bit, around shifts, lectures or that kitchen table.

The week starting 5th October has 19 of them, from Chinese pole to contemporary dance by way of trampoline and handstands.

The classes change week to week, so keep checking the schedule – maybe not as often as a Glasgow weather forecast, but often enough to match your mood and whether your productivity could do with a boost.

Most classes run for 90 minutes, which is a fair bit longer than a tea break and considerably more interesting to explain afterwards. But it's not like you've gone to the pub. And you can always cite the published scientific study to your boss (if you can't find someone to move your mouse occasionally while you're out.)

By 3pm you may have more tabs open. You might not mind quite so much.

## THE FLIGHTLESS FLY CLASS

## THE GROUNDWORK THAT POWERS EVERY SWING & TRICK

High divers describe their sport as primarily aerial rather than aquatic, which is a bold position for people who spend so much of their time fully submerged. They have a point, though. They get better faster by training their dives on dry land, on trampolines, dry boards and in harnesses, because (a) they can break the dive into smaller parts and (b) they can repeat each part many more times than if they had to swim to the side and clamber up the ladder every time.

Circus Fitness: Flying Trapeze is the flyers' version of all that, on Wednesdays from 6pm-7pm with Mark and Gabe. It involves the trampoline, getting used to moving through the air and making the right shapes without all the complications of your swing. There are dish and arch drills because your tricks depend on those two shapes. And there's the leg lifts and pull-ups that every swing depends on, therefore every trick too.

Only for one thing will you get on the fly rig: kill and builds, everyone's least-most favourite form of endurance torture. It's that part when people watching find themselves swaying in rhythm with the one on the bar, like a row of cartoon meerkats, urging the flyer onwards and upwards.

Nea's Fly Fundamentals on Tuesdays covers some of this ground, but you also drill your swings and turnarounds on the fly rig.

When you do want to practice tricks, Thursday is now the only one day of the week when you can't – unless it turns up on the daytime schedule, that is.

Saturday morning has two flying trapeze classes in a row, at 10am and 11.30am, so the weekend can start a few metres high and stay there until lunch. You can do another double after brunch on Sunday: classes at 1pm and 2.30pm.

If you do the groundwork, your airtime will get noticeably better. Guaranteed by Mark and Gabe, who have lived through this kind of training regimen.

## YOUTH CIRCUS

## HALF-TERM YOUTH CIRCUS CAMP CHANGES THE ANSWER

Most days, the journey home from school runs on a well-worn script. You ask what they did that day, and they tell you nothing, with the conviction of someone who has been doing nothing very hard since 9am.

Half-term has a way of breaking the script. The answer at pick-up from Youth Circus Camp is more likely to involve an enthusiastic description of which way up they spent the morning and afternoon, plus there may be a new word or two they'll use before dinner whether or not anyone asks, and it will be the name of a move they tried.

Open Rig Night starts with Youth Circus students in the spotlight on the Saturday before camp, so any camper who was on stage will arrive on Monday morning with rather a lot to report before the warm-up even gets started.

By Friday the script will have turned round completely. The last half hour of camp is an informal showing for family and friends, and you finally get to see what nothing looks like.
