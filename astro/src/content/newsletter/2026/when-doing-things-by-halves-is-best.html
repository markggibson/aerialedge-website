HALF OF IT, TWICE AS GOOD

WHAT DOUBLES TRAPEZE TAUGHT ME ABOUT THE SOLO ONES

I'm teaching doubles trapeze at October Edge this year, and I love it, because doubles is the discipline that changed how I think about all the others.

I originally started with solo static trapeze and within six months I was performing doubles in my first act with two other people, on two static trapezes.

I found it much easier to perform with two people than by myself. There are so many more options for storytelling when you have someone to work with.

Because the audience is watching two people depending on each other, doubles carries narrative and feeling that a solo piece has to work harder to achieve.

On a doubles bar you are visibly half of what's happening. A shape only exists if both people hold their end of it. You can be strong and clean and have it still not work, because the other person is having a different day and you read it too late.

That sounds like a weakness in the form. I've come to think it's the point of it. Once you've trained something that only works as a pair, you notice how little of circus is truly solo.

Someone spots your first inversion. Someone stands under the silks and tells you what the shape looked like from the floor, because you can't see it from inside it. Someone being in the room at all can be mostly why you try a hard thing.

I took a walk back in time when I was at the Fringe, to enjoy the magic of partership when seeing my teachers, Alex and Charlotte from Ockham's Razor, in Collaborator, the show they are touring.

It was wonderful to see their connection and ability to tell stories, one of the original inspirations for me in the first place.

Come and be half of something. It's a full-on experience.

Mark Gibson
Aerial Edge Founder

THE APPARATUS THAT WON'T HOLD STILL

AND THAT'S HALF THE FUN OF FLY POLE WITH ELKE

Every apparatus you've trained so far has done you one favour: it stayed more or less still while you gripped it, unless you chose to make it spin.

Fly pole doesn't. It spins whether you make it happen or not. Get on it and it turns under you, so half of learning it is doing the thing you meant to do while the pole has other ideas.

It maintains the challenge even when you're in Elke's advanced workshop. The tricks get trickier and the pole can keep tricking you, but you get more wise to it.

With Elke's guidance, you'll hit some cracking shapes and most of all in her Creative Signature Tricks workshop. The content in this is exceptional – things Elke rarely teaches, featuring tricks inspired by ballet and contemporary dance.

Open to everyone from high beginner up, Elke will show you unusual contact points and spin-held shapes that she files somewhere between elegant and outright weird. There's only one place left in the beginner workshop so if that fills, Creative Signature Tricks is the one to go for.

There's no weekly fly pole class on the timetable. This Saturday is the last workshop day until the New Year, so if it's the apparatus you keep watching other people do, this is the window – beginner slot included.

WHY THE BEST THINGS ARE DONE BY HALVES

DOUBLES TRAPEZE, AND THE SUNDAY CLASS THAT KEEPS MOMENTUM GOING

Mark is teaching doubles trapeze as one of the October Edge tracks – three days on the apparatus with the same group, long enough to get past the awkwardness of introductions and into real work.

And there's now a weekly class. Static solo and doubles trapeze, every Sunday, so what you start at Edge is something you carry on rather than shelve until the next one-off.

Doubles earns the effort. On a solo apparatus, strong and clean mostly gets you there. On a doubles bar it doesn't, because a shape only holds if both people hit the right place at the right moment.

You stop only managing your own body and start reading and anticipating a second one. Solo work doesn't teach balance, counter-balance, or lifts in the air, so there are many more moves to play with.

Trust is not a romantic slogan

Counterweighting is a recognised specialism in its own right. Two bodies can hold positions, and fall into and out of them, in ways a single aerialist can't. The two people are part of the structure, it's not that one is decoration.

You'll come to understand the value of constant communication under load. Verbal and physical cues for timing are crucial while you're both working hard and off the ground.

In doubles, trust is literal, not a romanticised slogan. The flyer commits to a lean or a drop knowing the only thing saving them is the base. Both of you need to understand and take responsibility for that.

This can push some buttons, and is a great learning experience for life too, because you meet yourself in the midst of your anxiety and understand what it takes to move through it to a positive outcome.

Canadian twin trapeze artists Karyne and Sarah Steben, who performed doubles trapeze for Cirque du Soleil's Saltimbanco, said the hard part for them was being exactly the same weight: no tidy base-and-flyer split, just two people working it out together.

Of course, that fact might also be the thrall of it. They are often credited as the first women to do feet-to-feet catching so it's obviously pushing their creativity.

Every mistake is a conversation

Karyne said in an interview in Cirque Fascination!: "We always knew we were on this planet together to give a message, to show how two people can use each other, trust each other, complement each other in their strength and weakness."

On solo trapeze you fail privately. On doubles, every mistake is a conversation, which opens doors to greater understanding and progress in your training and life.

DID YOU CHOOSE YOUR APPARATUS TOO SOON?

A CHANCE TO TRY EVERYTHING, TO FIND OUT WHAT FITS

Most people arrive at circus and pick a lane fairly quickly. You try a taster, or a friend does hoop, or silks is the class with space that term – and that becomes your apparatus. You get good at it. You also stop meeting the others.

The Four-Week Intensive Course is the stretch where you get to grips with all of them properly, back-to-back from 10am til 5pm every weekday. Plus you get the conditioning and self-awareness of physiology that lets you train that much without falling apart.

With this course, you can come in with whatever you already have – absolutely nothing, a year of one class, a bit of everything. Whatever.

What you leave with is a better idea of which disciplines your body actually loves, reached by working through the lot. For some people that points them at the Foundation Course. For others it points back to their weekly class, with a different plan for it.

The Four-Week Intensive ends with you performing a solo act you've made at an informal showcase. You can show your work to your family, friends and whoever is in the school on the last afternoon, like Domitilla in the photo above, from the July course.

YOU DON'T HAVE TO BE READY

DARING GREATLY IS WHAT COUNTS

There's a version of performing that is so polished that it's in danger of extinction before the spotlight ever picks it out of the darkness. You'll do it once the act is finished. Once that particular skill is clean every time. Once you feel ready.

That version doesn't really arrive. Open Rig Night is for the one that does.

You can call it anything from perfectionism to making excuses, but there's usually a fear sitting behind the constantly moving criteria for going ahead with the thing that you've created.

Could be fear of what people will say/think about it/you. Could be fear that you don't have enough time to practice. All sorts of fears, and usually at the root it's some form of 'not good enough'.

It's valid. It takes a bit of courage to get out there in front of an audience. It's a common if not universal experience, most people feel the fear. And the point is, the fear will only go away when you do it.

Embody the power of vulnerability

As Brené Brown explains so well in her work on courage and vulnerability, the fear is the whole point, and so is the triumph on the other side of it. It's not the critic who counts. It's the person who's up there, daring greatly.

Daring Greatly is the title of Brené's book because she so admired the phrase from US President Theodore Roosevelt's 1910 speech about the difference between doing anything and watching it from the stands (condensed version in the graphic above).

She's spent her career researching shame, and found it's closely linked to vulnerability: the fear of shame is exactly what makes you feel vulnerable about doing the thing in the first place. That's normal. Daring greatly is the cure. Her Ted Talk, The Power of Vulnerability, is worth a watch for inspiration. It's the 4th most-watched Ted Talk of all-time.

No one we know who's ever performed at Open Rig Night has regretted it. They probably all felt the fear.

You're not alone

So don't worry that you don't quite feel ready, and don't worry about what the audience might think (they'll be appreciative though, they always are). This is for you, for your personal development – for the amazing feeling of achievement on the other side of it. You're the one who did it, no one can take that away from you.

You might find something works better on the night than it ever has in class. You might find a bit that needs rethinking. Both are worth knowing.

You won't be sorting your act out alone. Your instructor can help you shape your idea. Mark and Yara are there for the nerves, the lighting, the logistics, and the part where you wonder what you've signed up for.
