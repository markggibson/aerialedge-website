## NEW AIRPORT IN GLASGOW

## FOUR WAYS TO TAKE OFF: DESTINATION UNKNOWN

Big news for Scottish aviation. From Friday 17 to Sunday 19 July, Glasgow has a second airport. It's at 240 Edmiston Drive, the departures board reads flying trapeze, cradle, teeterboard and the fabulous acrobatic bar.

That's the whole idea of the Summer Festival: four circus arts that belong together because they all feature a take-off.

Flying trapeze is the scheduled service you already know. Teeterboard is a seesaw with ambition — when someone is landing at one terminal, someone else is taking off from the other terminal.

The acrobatic bar is a long, thin, flexy plank that you stand on, while it's balanced on the shoulders of two bases. When you've boarded properly, it fires you skyward.

And cradle is the one you know, but need to do more of. Sometimes you fly to the catcher who's hanging by their knees, arms out, ready for you. It's the closest thing circus has to being met at arrivals. And sometimes you take off from the cradle itself while holding tight to the catcher.

We've scheduled the new flights around the flying trapeze classes you already enjoy on Fridays and Sundays.

Your aerial multi-class pass is your boarding pass. It gets you into any festival session. Want the full itinerary, Friday night through Sunday? The All Access Festival Pass covers all ten workshops at £15 each — business class at budget prices.

The Acro Jam runs all day Saturday, free for anyone on a festival workshop. Think of it as the departure lounge, except fun.

From this airport, every flight leaves exactly on time. Where it goes is up to you.

Summer Festival
Friday 17th July til Sunday 19th
Drop-in £25 per workshop or do 10 for £15 each. Aerial multi-class passes can be used.

## SEE IT,  LOVE IT,  DO IT

## COMMONWEALTH GAMES FESTIVAL FREE SHOW + TASTER CLASSES

This summer we let circus out to play. Three times it went roaming through Glasgow's shopping centres, from Govan Cross and beyond – and last week we rocked up to Silverburn shopping mall.

Now we're turning the whole thing around. Glasgow is invited to ours.

For the grand finale of our Commonwealth Games Festival events, we're staging a free show here at Aerial Edge, and the cast is the school's entire family tree – instructors, graduates of our full-time courses, adult students who train in the evenings, and Youth Circus students.

When the show ends, the performers head for the teaching floor, where they and others from the Aerial Edge instructor team will run free 30-minute taster classes for everyone aged 7 and up. Under-7s are very welcome to come and watch, and to start plotting.

The whole idea behind our Circus For All events has been simple – get close enough and you realise ordinary people are doing this, so you could too.

That's what happened big-time at Silverburn, the last of our pop-up events in the wild. When spectators see kids launching themselves into the activities, they think it's just for kids...but the temptation gets the better of them, and they join in too. Quite right!

So bring friends. These Commonwealth Games Festival events are to encourage new people to give it a go.

It's worth considering as you watch the show on the 25th, that every single one of the performers started out in an audience, watching somebody else fly.

One word to the wise. Taster places can be booked in advance, and they will be. You may come as a spectator and decide on the day you want to try it, but by then the spots may be spoken for. If someone you know has spent years wondering whether circus is for them, bring them — and book their place before somebody quicker does.

## AND THEN THE OTHER SIDE!

## NEA ON SILKS, ROPE, AND WHY 'I CAN'T' NEVER LASTS LONG

People tell Nea they can't do rope or silks all the time. It's one of her favourite parts of teaching, because it usually stops being true fast. "Sometimes within the same class, even," she says.

The secret, she says, is that there's no single way up. "It's all about picking the right progressions and cues for each person, and that's what I enjoy about teaching. I don't always get it right, but I do try my best to find what works for everyone."

Her classes are built on strong basics, with conditioning woven through every session, and on rope especially she loves teaching dynamics. "They can be tricky to begin with, but once they start making sense they open so many doors. They also make you feel really cool, especially when you get your first release-catch trick."

If open level classes have ever felt intimidating, watching what everyone else is working on, Nea reads the room the other way round. "The teachers and regulars know how hard the moves you're learning are, and they're just as excited to see the progress you're making. If not more, especially when it comes to me."

You don't need to arrive strong either. Building strength outside class helps movement in the air feel easier and keeps injuries away, but Nea says: "You don't need much to start off with – it comes with practice". Most people feel stronger and more confident sooner than they expect.

One warning before you book. Everything in Nea's classes happens twice...you may get bored of hearing "and then the other side!" very quickly. But your left side will thank her eventually!

## STRENGTH OR STRETCH TO PREVENT INJURY?

## AND OTHER QUESTIONS YOUR BODY KEEPS ASKING

How best to prevent injury is the kind of question in a training space that can start a hotter debate than politics at an activist rally. Ask ten circus artists and you could get ten different answers, usually delivered with some conviction.

We won't subject you to clickbait frustration, so here's the answer: strength wins.  Mark's been digging into the research — large-scale reviews covering thousands of participants. Stretching alone, for all the hours we may lovingly give it, does less for injury prevention than you might think, but it's still an integral part of a good programme.

Mark's new blog tells you how big the strength effect actually is, what kind of strength work counts, where stretching earns its keep in your training...and one finding that will annoy anyone who trains to failure.

## IS IRATA WORTH IT FOR CIRCUS RIGGERS?

## PROFESSIONAL RIGGING CERTIFICATION, DECODED

It's been a while since we mentioned circusrigging.info, so a quick reintroduction is in order.

It's our sister site, where Mark and other experts write about the technical skills that keep circus in the air.

There's plenty on there that's free to read, because safety standards hold up only as well as the information behind them, and good information should be easy to find. The more technical info is available for a very small subscription fee (£5 per month or £50 a year).

The latest article takes on a question working riggers often ask themselves: is IRATA – the industrial rope access certification – worth it for circus?

And, so you're not left dangling, here's the answer: yes, for part of the job.

IRATA teaches you to work safely on ropes at height, using doubled-up rope systems where no single failure can hurt you, and it teaches that very well. What it doesn't teach is how to rig a circus act – performer loads, shock loading, apparatus. You need to learn that separately.

The full article gives you the lowdown on what each of the three IRATA levels actually covers, what a Level 1 course costs, the 1,000-hour catch people don't mention upfront, and the safety statistic that explains why industry swears by the two-rope system.

If rigging is any part of your circus life, give it a read.

## THE CONFIDENCE FACTORY

## WHAT YOUTH CIRCUS SUMMER CAMP QUIETLY BUILDS

If you could stand at the back of the room on Monday and again on Friday, you'd see it. On Monday, someone hesitates at the bottom of the silks. By Friday, they're negotiating for one more go. A trick that looked impossible on day one has become part of the warm-up, and a kid who arrived a little unsure is standing a little taller.

That's why we've nicknamed Summer Camp The Confidence Factory. You won't find confidence classes anywhere on the timetable – we teach climbs, catches, balances and drops. Confidence is what gets made while they're busy having fun.

Sure, it's to do with the hours in the air, but it's also to do with the 'have a go' spirit when young people get together in a room full of possibilities, and the freedom to approach the task in the way that works best for them.

They notice that people go about things in different ways, and that it's not just okay, it's encouraged. It's not an 'anything goes' situation because safety comes first, so they learn how to assess risk - that's another thing that doesn't show up on a standard curriculum.

The Confidence Factory opens on Monday, with just a few spots left. Just as well there's another camp week from August 3rd – and by August, a summer's worth of energy will need somewhere to go.
