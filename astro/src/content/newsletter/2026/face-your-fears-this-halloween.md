## MONSTERS UNDER THE CRASH MAT

## SOME THINGS ARE MUCH LESS SCARY ONCE YOU'VE FACED THEM

Halloween is that glorious time when we indulge ourselves in our favourite fears, and circus has its own collection of monsters, some in the mind, some real.

Some people queue for ghost trains, watch films through their fingers and put small children in skeleton costumes, then give them sweets for approaching strangers in the dark.

For us, it's more about finding difficult ways to go upside down, letting go when your mind urges you to hang on, moving at speed through tricky manoeuvres, walking into a class where you feel everyone else will obviously be stronger, bendier and more experienced than you, looking stupid, and discovering that you are possibly not naturally as gifted as you'd hoped at the thing you have just had the courage to try. These are all scary things.

The trick is to learn which fears are giving you useful information and which are meaningless bumps in the night. Risk deserves respect, that's why we use progressions, mats, lines, spotting, rigging and judgement. But fears of our feelings when we're new, imperfect or watched have a habit of shrinking once you actually bring them into the light to look at them.

So if there is something at Aerial Edge you have been avoiding because it's too scary, Halloween season seems an appropriate time to investigate the monster. It may turn out to be less awful than you'd imagined, once you get to know it.

Mark Gibson
Aerial Edge Founder

## BE CONSCIOUS OF YOUR CREATIVITY

## LETTING YOUR IDEAS OUT IS CHILD'S PLAY

What would you rather – be able to let your imagination generate wild and wonderful acro moves but be anxious about sharing them, or be less creative but more confident about what you want to do?

There's a brand new [study](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/4945f103705?contactId={{contact.id}}&id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0346553&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing) published this month which blows up the belief that kids are more imaginative than adults. But it comes with a kicker that's worth thinking about before acro jam.

Researchers gave 92 preschool children and 70 adults a series of idea-generating and drawing tasks, then had independent judges rate the results for novelty, effectiveness and overall creativity. The adults did better, particularly when the task had a clear goal.

So much for the naturally free-range, creative child and the grown-up who's grown out of imagination games.

Except the children had something else going for them. They reported less anxiety on a timed ideas task and enjoyed two of the three tasks more.

Which raises the possibility that adulthood leaves us with plenty of ideas and a very efficient internal heckler.

So, you might want to consider replacing those voices in your head with new assertions: I am creative, my idea could work and it will be fun to try, other people will want to help...and so on.

Acro Jam is three hours in which your unfinished ideas are allowed out in public. Who knows, one day it might end up being in a performance like Julia and Robyn in the photo above, taken at Open Rig Night this month.

There is no curriculum at acro jams, and plenty of thought-sharing with other participants. Bring a semi-steady handstand, a half-formed lift, a transition you want other bodies to solve with you, or simply the desire to see what happens in one of these sessions.

The tumble track, soft acro floor and safety harness with lines are there for the experiments, and Robyn is hosting. It goes ahead once 10 people have booked, so book by noon on Friday 6th November.

You do not have to arrive with something clever. In fact, the research suggests you may already have plenty of clever. What you need is this chance to play with it before your inner preschooler gets outvoted.

## YOU LEARNT THE IMPORTANT BIT ON A PLAYGROUND SWING

## THE PHYSICS OF DYNAMIC STRAPS WITHOUT SIMPLY TRYING HARDER

Long before most of us knew what an oscillation was, we had already carried out a fairly sophisticated physics experiment in a playground.

Put a child on a swing and they discover that flinging their body around at random is not the route to the sky. Movement has to happen at the right point in the swing.

Dynamic straps brings us back to that delicious problem. Freya's workshop covers beats, turns, dynamic pathways into static shapes, alternate-grip beats including elbow and chest beats, and switching grips while you are in the air. There are different pathways for different levels, but the common ingredient is learning what the swing is giving you and what to do with it.

The good news is that your body may remember a lot of what you need to do in dynamic straps, from your playground days. We know this because a 2005 [study](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/951ab2f36a4?contactId={{contact.id}}&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing) modelled standing and squatting on a swing and asked whether children pump it in the fastest way.

There are A LOT of fancy physics equations and talk of "Pontryagin’s Minimum Principle and geometric techniques" in the published paper, which some may say draws a conclusion like a memo from the Government Department of the Bleedin' Obvious: kids know how to go as high and fast as possible.

But it does contain this glorious phrase: "Humans while performing skilled tasks act as self-optimising machines." Apparently it's a key concept of biomechanics and the playground kids proved it with their swing technique.

Sooo, there's every reason to believe that you may already know more about dynamics than you realise, if you ever played in a swing park as a kid. You are a self-optimising machine, after all.

The workshop is open to people with some straps experience, circus fitness classes included, and to aerialists who already work dynamically on hoop, rope or flying trapeze and fancy finding out what transfers to a different piece of kit.

Freya calls it "the sheer joy of swinging through the air". The physicists can keep their equations, the joy is what it's all about.

## TWO PEOPLE, ONE HOOP, MANY MORE POSSIBILITIES

## FREYA SHOWS YOU HOW TO DUET

Aerial can be a wonderfully self-contained relationship: you, the apparatus and whatever argument the two of you are having that day. Add another person to the apparatus and the vocabulary changes.

In Freya Pellie's Doubles Hoop workshop, you'll work through duo synchro choreography in ways that keep the language clean. It's a surprisingly detailed business, staying in time with another human. Then you'll move into base-flyer tricks where one person lifts or assists the other.

That creates shapes you simply can't make alone, but it also changes what you have to pay attention to. Your partner has weight, timing, momentum and opinions of their own. You have to feel what they are doing as well as know what you are doing.

Freya knows that relationship from the performing side. She is a multidisciplinary circus artist specialising in hoop and rope, has performed and taught across the UK and abroad. Her recent duo hoop work includes show runs with Cirquoise and Hippodrome Circus as part of The Virago Sisters.

You do not, however, need your own Sister to book. Come with a friend if you want to, or sign up solo and meet people in class. The workshop is for strong beginners to intermediate aerialists.

There is a whole world of aerial that only exists when there are two of you in it.

## A GIANT THAT MOVES WITH YOU

## ELKE HAS FALLEN IN LOVE WITH FLY POLE...THIS MAY BE CONTAGIOUS.

Elke Bucher knows what it feels like to work against a pole. On Chinese pole and chrome pole, the apparatus stays where it is and you create the movement: pushing hard, placing everything exactly and accepting, in Elke's words, that "sheer pain" cannot always be avoided.

Then she met flying pole.

"A flying pole moves WITH you," she says. It shifts through push and pull, pivots around you through front-to-back spins, and "glides into position as gently as a big silicone giant can do".

It can also go very fast, so you do need to hold on tight for the ride. Elke plans to use more of the space for the first time for big, circus-arena-style spins in her fly pole workshops on Saturday 21st November. That spatial freedom is one of the things she loves most about the apparatus.

Her favourite tricks are Pegasus and dragontail: shapes she keeps returning to to find new entries, pathways and transitions, including, for these workshops, ideas about "how to survive these when going faaast".

There is work involved. Flying pole is tougher on the bottom arm than regular pole, so Elke builds breaks between heavy pushes into the sessions and will share conditioning advice, particularly for flying-pole newcomers.

She also has an affection for tricks with awkward contact points because, once you have made friends with them, they stay with you. "It feels like having won a bit of a battle of the elements somehow," she says.

## YOUR HEART KNOWS WHEN IT'S LIVE

## WHY WE'RE ALREADY PUTTING THESE ROOTS OF OURS IN YOUR DIARY

Researchers have just found a way of measuring something that circus and theatre people have been known to be true since forever: being at a live performance makes your heart react in a way that just doesn't happen when you're watching on screen. That's got to be good for you, right?

There's something special about being there with the performers. Which is what we want you to know in anticipation of the show that's being created right now by our Foundation Course students, with Director Michelle Ross, These Roots of Ours.

This was discovered when nearly 300 people at two different concerts had electrodes strapped on their chests by to monitor heart activity, then they were randomly sent either into the auditorium or into another room to watch a professionally filmed livestream of the same concert, at exactly the same time. The auditorium audience reported feeling more immersed than the livestreamers, but more intriguingly, their heart rates were more in step with one another, researchers reported in the prestigious scientific journal, [Nature](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/ce44c5a4cf0?contactId={{contact.id}}&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing) this June.

We can't tell you much about what These Roots of Ours will look like yet, because show creation is exactly that: it's still in the making. But the evidence from previous ensemble productions with our full-time students is that your heart will thank you for bringing it along for the experience.

Right now, this group of circus artists are working out what an idea which now has a title means to them in bodies, apparatus, individual acts and the moments when they all occupy the stage together.

That's what makes a Foundation Course show different from a collection of acts on Open Rig Nights. It's not one person at a time pouring their heart and soul into their act, it's all of them in sync, feeling the concept together. Tenner's bet performers' heartbeats would align at least as much as the audience's if that experiment was to be done.

Michelle's job is to draw out what each performer can bring and help the company find the thing they can make together that none of them could make alone.

And then it needs one final component: you.

We'll tell you more about These Roots of Ours as it grows. For now, put the show date in your diary. Your heart may know why it's good to be there before the rest of you does.

## KIDS HOLD SECRET TO SUCCESS

## THEY'RE MORE CONFIDENT ABOUT CREATIVITY THAN ADULTS

The mildly shocking news that adults produced more highly rated creative work than children has been floated in the acro jam article in this newsletter. For Youth Circus parents, it's worth focusing on the fact that children have a secret sauce to success.

The 92 preschoolers in the newly published [study](https://api.aerialedge.co.uk/email-tracking/4945f103705?contactId={{contact.id}}&id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0346553&utm_source=email&utm_medium=email marketing) felt more confident about their creativity than the adults did, and were more motivated on two of the three tasks.

That feels familiar if you spend time around children learning circus. Give them an apparatus, a movement problem or permission to invent something and they are often considerably less interested than adults in whether the first attempt makes them look competent.

They try things. They repeat them. They turn them into something else. They show somebody. They look at somebody else trying it a different way. Then they try the next thing.

It doesn't mean that children never get self-conscious, frustrated or worried about getting things wrong. Of course they do. But the study raises an interesting question for the grown-ups around them – parents and teachers. Our job includes nurturing that confidence, so that they take it into adulthood. Then creativity and unabashed sharing of ideas could be their superpower in whatever life they choose to live.

Preschooler Lydia and her mum Joanne, in the photo above from Open Rig Night this month, must be doing something right. They are both regular attendees at our Monday class for under-7s, and Lydia was planning this performance before their first act was staged at the spring Open Rig Night!

At Youth Circus, skills, strength, technique and safe progression all matter, and so does keeping hold of the part that says: what happens if I try this? We reward effort and enthusiasm more than achievement.

## LET'S DO DAY SHIFTS

## DAY CLASSES NOW RUNNING EVERY WEEK

Two o'clock on a Wednesday is a strange hour. Lunch has been consumed, the morning's ambitions have been surreptitiously revised, and the afternoon is stretching out like a cat that needs to be given something to do. From Wednesday, flying trapeze can slide into your schedule.

The new 90-minute class with Hamish is open level, which means that if you have never held a trapeze bar in your life, you are in the right room and nobody will ask you to prove you've done anything to deserve it.

Keep an eye on the daytime schedule on TeamUp or Instagram, there's a strong chance of Handstands on Mondays at 11.30am, and other classes could show up before the next newsletter.

Most people fit a hobby in around work and dinner, so a weekday asks for a slightly different approach. Your week might have a gap in it already, or a day off, or you might decide to shuffle your schedule to make space for daytime circus. It's certainly worth doing.

For an hour and a half, the most pressing item on your to-do list becomes letting go of a bar at the right moment. As essential as you may be in whatever you do, your inbox probably won't miss you and the world will keep turning. And when you return to your post, you'll be refreshed and delighted with your escape, so you'll be better placed to keep everything going smoothly.
