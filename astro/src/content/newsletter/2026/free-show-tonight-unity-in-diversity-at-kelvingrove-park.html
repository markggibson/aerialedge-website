FALLING IS NOT FAILING

GETTING UP IS THE WHOLE POINT

Nana korobi ya oki. It's not a greeting from a cheerfully swaying Glaswegian, it's an old Japanese proverb: fall down seven times, get up eight.

Everyone in last Saturday's Commonwealth Games Festival show has lived experience of that proverb, they've all learned through their circus journey that failing at the first few attempts isn't failing at all. And four-year-old Lydia, performing with mum Joanne, led the way.

The people who tried our circus taster classes for the first time straight after the show got the idea inside their half-hour sessions. We hope to see more of them at the rig, falling down figuratively, and getting back up to try again.

The cast of the show ranged from Lydia through former students turned Aerial Edge instructors, to students performing for the first time, and at least one person who's been drawing their pension for years 👀.

They've all been through the phase of falling off things, looking clumsy in front of people, and they kept coming back anyway. That's the part of the proverb that actually matters. Everyone falls. What counts is getting up again on purpose, often enough that it stops being falling and starts being practice.

Mark Gibson
Aerial Edge Founder

HOW TO GET YOUR FRONT SPLIT

STEP-BY-STEP GUIDE IN THE BLOG

A year of pigeon pose, and your front split looks pretty much like it did when you started. Most advice can't explain why. It hands out the same three stretches whether you're tight or not, and never asks what's actually stopping you.

Our new blog starts with a five-minute check instead, before you stretch a single muscle. Is it tightness, or weakness in the exact position you're reaching for? Is your front leg ready while your back leg isn't, or the other way round?

From there, it's practical – which stretches match which limiter, which strength work closes the gap, and how to tell a stretch that's working from one that's just familiar.

Train the challenge that's actually there, not a routine by rote.

EVERYONE'S BEGINNING SOMETHING

4-WEEK INTENSIVE, FC & PDP ALL START THE SAME MORNING

You know that feeling when you walk to a room and everyone seems to know each other, and what to do, so you hover at the door wondering if it's too late to still be a beginner in there? Rest assured, it's not too late at Aerial Edge. Never.

At the start of each full-time course, and plenty of classes, it's full of people exactly as new to it as you'd be.

Everyone comes with different life experiences, some with skills in physical activities that could be dance, circus, theatre - and some come with none of these. But they're all meant to be there, and they'll all finish with a skillset and experience that's unique to them.

The Four-Week Intensive is where you start when you want to try training all day every day, or when you can't commit any more time to it. By the end of it you'll know things about your own body you didn't know a month before. Some people finish there and go back to their old life, a new layer of experiences to colour their day-to-day tasks.

Others want more. The Four-Week Intensive runs alongside the start of the Foundation Course, so you're already in the room and can simply keep going. Those 16 weeks are transformative, building toward an ensemble show with a professional director, performing something you helped make.

Go further again and the Professional Development Programme picks up from there. Seven more weeks with specialists in act creation, character work, lighting and projection, ending with a professionally shot showreel that's actually yours to use.

Nobody in that room started already good at everything, they were all there to learn.

DON'T CUT THIS TAG OFF

WHAT THE BREAKING LOAD NUMBER ACTUALLY MEANS, AND DOESN'T

You know the tag on a new jumper, the one you cut off the second you get home because it's itchy and nobody needs to know your size? Do NOT do that with rigging kit, no matter how annoying it is.

Every rope, strop and piece of rigging hardware carries a tag, and unlike your jumper's, this one's not just about the materials used. It has essential details about how you can use it, and you need to reference it for regular inspections.

Here's the part worth slowing down for...don't take the number on it entirely at face value.

Some people picture a Minimum Breaking Load rating as the exact force at which one unlucky sample snapped in a lab somewhere. It isn't.

The newest article on circusrigging.info explains what it really is – a manufacturer tests a whole batch of the same part to failure, works out the mean breaking force across that batch, then subtracts three standard deviations from it. That's the three-sigma method, and whatever's left over is the number stamped on the tag.

It's a deliberately pessimistic number. Because it sits three standard deviations below average, almost every individual part actually breaks at a higher force than its own rating, sometimes a lot higher. That margin isn't bolted on afterward. It's built into the maths from the start.

12 minutes of reading, and you'll never look at a tag the same way again, let alone take it off.
